Cooking in a camper: good food on two flames

Guide · 7 min. reading time

⏱️ No time? Here is the summary

The camper kitchen rewards simplicity: few, good utensils instead of fully equipped, one-pot dishes as standard, fresh and regional purchases at markets. In good weather, the kitchen moves outside (less heat, odor, and moisture in the van), washing is done using the two-bowl method and biodegradable dish soap. Cooking with the lid saves gas — and a fire blanket should be within reach.

Two flames, thirty centimeters of workspace, a minibar-sized refrigerator — and yet I swear to you: I often eat better on the go than at home. No contradiction! The camper kitchen demands simplicity, and simplicity is the foundation of good cuisine. Here, I'll show you how to organize your onboard kitchen, what really works on the go, and which tricks can turn limited equipment into a lot of enjoyment. 💛

Understanding the onboard kitchen: what it can and cannot do

A typical camper kitchen offers two to three gas burners, a small sink, little workspace, and a refrigerator between 60 and 140 liters. An oven is the exception, the microwave luxury. In addition, there are three silent players who influence every cooking decision: the limited Water supply, the limited gas supply and the fact that cooking fumes remain in the small interior — as warmth, smell, and moisture.

This results in the three basic rules of camper cooking: cook simply (few pots, few ingredients), cook with a lid (saves gas and reduces steam), and cook outdoors whenever possible.

The right equipment: less, but better

The experience of thousands of campers can be summed up in a short list: a large and a small pot, a well-coated pan, a sharp knife, a sturdy cutting board, spatula, cooking spoon, sieve, and a bowl suitable for stirring, serving, and washing. Plus stackable storage containers and reusable bags.

Three extras deserve their place: one Omnia oven or a comparable countertop oven for the gas flame — it bakes bread, casseroles, and cakes and greatly expands the repertoire; a Bialetti or a hand filter for real coffee; and a Outdoor stove or compact gas grill for the outdoor kitchen. What, on the other hand, almost always goes unused: special equipment, the third cutting board, and dishes "for guests" who never show up. 😄

One-Pot: The Principle of Success

One-pot dishes are the kings of camper cuisine: a flame, minimal washing, maximum flavor. The principle: sauté onions and vegetables, add liquid and starch side dish (pasta, rice, couscous, lentils) directly, cook with the lid on, season to taste, done. On this basis, countless variations are created — Mediterranean with tomato and olives, oriental with chickpeas and cumin, Asian with coconut milk and curry.

Also proven: stir-fries made from the local market, shakshuka for breakfast or dinner, wraps and bowls made from prepared ingredients, soups in the cold season. And for the weekend: fresh bread from the countertop oven — the smell alone is worth the appliance.

Shopping regionally: the pantry is called a travel destination

Perhaps the most beautiful aspect of camper cuisine: your pantry changes with every country you travel. Weekly markets, farm shops, and fish stalls supply ingredients that are fresher and often cheaper than any supermarket — and from the same pasta dish, local vegetables, cheese, or fish becomes a new travel experience every time. In Poland, I regularly went through the weekly markets — just the pierogi ingredients alone are worth a trip. 😉

So deliberately plan little ahead: A basic stock of durable items goes along (pasta, rice, strained tomatoes, oil, spices, canned goods for emergencies), and all fresh items are bought locally every two or three days. By the way, this keeps the fridge tidy — important, because a fully packed fridge cools poorly, and with absorber devices, you also need a somewhat horizontal vehicle to deliver power.

Cooking outside: the summer rule

As soon as the weather allows, the kitchen moves outside. There are good reasons for this: roasting and braising inside heats up the camper, spreads odors to upholstery and curtains, and settles as a fat-moisture film on surfaces. A gas stove on the camping table or a compact grill solves all of that — and makes cooking a sociable part of the evening.

Two restrictions: open fires and sometimes even gas grills are forbidden in many places in dry regions and summer months — inform yourself and respect prohibitions without exception. And with wind, the stove belongs in the windshield, otherwise half the gas evaporates ineffectively.

Save water: the dishwashing school

Water is the scarcest resource on board, and dishwashing is its biggest consumer. The tried-and-true routine: Coarsely wipe plates and pans with kitchen paper right after eating (this also prevents grease in the greywater tank), then wash in a bowl with little hot dishwater, rinse briefly in a second bowl, and dry. This means a complete meal for four people uses just a few liters. Biodegradable dish soap is standard — for the sake of the greywater tank and the environment.

Saving gas works similarly unspectacularly: put the lid on, boil water only in the amount needed, let pasta soak with residual heat after boiling.

Safety: small but important

When cooking indoors, always open a window or the roof hatch — the flame consumes oxygen and produces water vapor. Never leave pots unattended, close the stove first, then the gas — and while driving, the flame is, of course, off. A firefighting ceiling within the kitchen's reach is part of the standard equipment.

Conclusion: The camper kitchen rewards everyone who values simplicity as strength: a pot, fresh regional ingredients, cooked outdoors, washed down with little water. Anyone who says goodbye to the idea of copying the home kitchen will realize: Cooking on the go is not the limited version of cooking — it's the relaxed one. And no kitchen in the world has a better view. 🌅
