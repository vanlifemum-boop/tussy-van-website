The camping etiquette: the unwritten rules among campers

Guide · 7 min. reading time

⏱️ No time? Here's the short version

The neighbor's plot is his garden - don't walk over it, don't park on it. Quiet times really apply, music remains at room volume. You leave the sanitary facilities as you want them to be, and the toilet cassette comes. exclusively to the disposal station. Dogs on a leash, bags in your hand. And when you stand free, you are an ambassador for all campers: inconspicuous, quiet, without a trace: behave as you would like your neighbors to behave.

Camping means living in a small space with lots of strangers - and yet it still works surprisingly harmoniously. This is due to an unwritten code of conduct that has developed over decades. No one is given it at check-in, but everyone expects to know it. I basically picked up camping with my mother's milk and never "learned" these rules - they were just always there. So that you don't have to laboriously figure them out through trial and error, I'll write them down for you in full here. 💛

Rule 1: The plot is holy ground

At the campsite, the plot replaces the home garden - and is treated in the same way. Specifically: You don't walk across other people's spaces, even if the detour to the sanitary building is annoying. You don't park halfway on your neighbor's property, don't put your chairs in their line of sight and don't use someone else's water or electricity without asking.

When you arrive, it is good manners to make yourself noticed with a greeting and to be considerate when setting up: if possible, do not align the side of the door or terrace directly with the neighbor's seating area, keep your distance, and do not let the awning protrude into someone else's territory. Children are of course allowed to play and run around - but it's best for them to learn early on that strange plots are not a shortcut.

Rule 2: Noise is the number one source of conflict

Nothing causes as much trouble on campsites as noise. Walls of campers, caravans and especially tents are acoustically practically non-existent. What you discuss at normal volume will be heard by the neighbor.

  • Rest times are binding. Night rest applies at almost all sites, many also have a midday rest period, during which even the barrier remains closed. The times are in the schedule - read them when checking in.
  • Music at room volume. Your taste in music is a private matter - in the literal sense. Bluetooth boxes on the meadow are the surest way to make yourself unpopular.
  • Late arrival, quiet setup. If you arrive after dark, position yourself as simply as possible, avoid long maneuvers with the engine running and postpone the fine adjustment until the morning.
  • Generator wisely. Electricity generators are forbidden in many places and are a nuisance in nature next to other campers. If you want to be self-sufficient, it's better to invest in solar and batteries.
  • Doors and flaps you can't beat it - especially not at six in the morning when you set off.

Rule 3: Treat shared facilities like your own bathroom

Sanitary blocks, sculleries and washrooms are shared by all guests. The golden rule: Leave them as you would like to find them. Take a quick shower (the mop in the bathroom is not a decoration 😉), remove hair from the drain, leave the sink clean - little things that make living together a lot easier.

A particularly sensitive topic is disposal:

  • The toilet cassette is only emptied at the designated disposal station for chemical toilets - never in normal toilets, sinks or even drains. Rinse thoroughly and leave the station clean.
  • Greywater (dishwashing and shower water) belongs in the designated floor inlet, not on the lawn or in the street drain.
  • Garbage is separated and placed in the containers - not placed next to full bins. And please never burn it in a campfire: plastic in a fire poisons the air and soil. Used diapers and wet wipes should only be disposed of in the residual waste, tightly closed.

Rule 4: Dogs on a leash, bag in hand

Camping with a dog is wonderful and widespread - as long as a few basic rules apply: respect the obligation to be on a leash on the site, do not leave the dog unattended and barking at the camper, put away shops always and everywhere and accept that not everyone loves dogs. Many places have their own dog showers and runs - use them.

Rule 5: Helpfulness and community

The camper community thrives on mutual help, and that's exactly what makes it so charming. Instructing when maneuvering, lending tools or a pinch of salt, pointing out the free space to newcomers, sharing tips about the region and route - all of this is a given. A friendly greeting as you pass by is just as important as a short chat in the evening if both parties like it.

A sense of the opposite direction is just as important: not everyone is looking for company. Anyone who reads their book or clearly wants some peace and quiet will get it. The art lies in the balance between openness and respect.

Rule 6: Protect nature - fire, food and wild animals

The proximity to nature is the best thing about camping. Let's make sure it stays that way:

  • Only take photos and memories with you: don't break branches, don't damage trees for firewood, don't pick rare plants.
  • Be careful with the fire: Barbecue and campfire bans apply without exception. Never leave a fire unattended and put it out completely before you sleep or leave.
  • Store food safely: Food smells attract wild animals. Food belongs in the vehicle or in fixed boxes - and wild animals are never fed, only observed from a safe distance.
  • Washing up & showering outside: stay at least 50 meters away from streams, rivers and lakes and use only minimal amounts of biodegradable soap.

Rule 7: When you stand freely you are an ambassador

Outside of official locations, you represent the entire camper guild - whether you want to or not. Any misbehavior pays into the “prohibition sign” account; any exemplary behavior keeps the doors open. That's why the stricter version of the etiquette applies here:

  • Stay inconspicuous: no furniture, no grill, no extended awning.
  • One night, then move on.
  • "Pack it in, pack it out": leave no rubbish or traces - if in doubt, take other people's rubbish with you.
  • Dispose of waste water and toilets only at official stations.
  • Greet residents in a friendly manner and respond without discussion when asked to continue driving.

Conclusion: Camping etiquette is not a rigid set of rules, but rather applied consideration: respect space, peace and quiet, be helpful without being intrusive, and behave outside in nature like a guest who wants to come back. Anyone who has this attitude doesn't need to learn a single rule by heart - and will be welcomed with open arms at every place in Europe.
