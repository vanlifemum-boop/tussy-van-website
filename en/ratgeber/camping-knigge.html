Camping etiquette: the unwritten rules among campers

Guide · 7 min. reading time

⏱️ No time? Here is the summary

The neighbor's plot is his garden — don't walk over it, don't park on it. Quiet times really apply; music stays at room volume. You leave the sanitary facilities exactly as you want them, and the toilet cassette arrives exclusively to the disposal station. Dogs on a leash, bags in hand. And when standing free, you are an ambassador for all campers: unobtrusive, quiet, without a trace. In short: behave the way you want your neighbors to behave.

Camping means living in a confined space with many strangers — and yet it works surprisingly harmoniously. This is due to an unwritten code of conduct that has developed over decades. No one receives it at check-in, but everyone expects to know it. I practically took camping with my mother's milk and never "learned" these rules — they were just always there. So you don't have to find them painstakingly through trial and error, I'm writing them down for you here in full. 💛

Rule 1: The plot is sacred ground

At the campsite, the plot replaces the home garden — and is treated the same way. Specifically: You don't cross other people's parking spaces, even if the detour to the sanitary house is annoying. You don't park half on the neighbor's property, don't put your chairs in its line of sight, and don't use foreign water or electricity without asking.

When arriving, it is good etiquette to make yourself heard with a greeting and to be considerate when setting up: avoid pointing the door and terrace side directly at the neighbor's seating area as much as possible, keep distance, and do not let the awning protrude into someone else's territory. Children are, of course, allowed to play and romp around — but they also learn early on that unfamiliar plots are not shortcuts.

Rule 2: Noise is the number one source of conflict

Nothing causes as much trouble at campsites as noise. Walls of campers, caravans, and especially tents are acoustically practically nonexistent. What you discuss at normal volume is heard by the neighbor.

  • Rest periods are binding. Nighttime rest applies at almost all places; many also have a midday rest, during which even the barrier remains closed. The times are listed in the seating schedule — read them at check-in.
  • Music at room volume. Your taste in music is a private matter — literally. Bluetooth speakers on the lawn are the safest way to make yourself unpopular.
  • Late arrival, quiet setup. Those arriving after dark can stand as simply as possible, avoid long maneuvering with engine running, and postpone fine-tuning until morning.
  • Generator thoughtfully. Power generators are banned in many places and are a nuisance in nature alongside other campers. Those who want to be self-sufficient are better off investing in solar and battery.
  • Doors and Tailgates you don't strike — especially not at six o'clock in the morning when leaving.

Rule 3: Treat communal facilities like your own bathroom

Sanitary facilities, dishwashing facilities, and washrooms are shared by all guests. The golden rule: leave them exactly as you want them to be. Briefly take a shower (the mop in the bathroom is not decoration 😉), remove hair from the drain, leave the sink clean — little things that make living together much easier.

A particularly sensitive topic is disposal:

  • The toilet cassette is emptied exclusively at the designated disposal station for chemical toilets — never in regular toilets, sinks, or even gutters. Rinse thoroughly and leave the station clean.
  • greywater (Rinse and shower water) belongs in the designated ground inlet, not in the meadow or the street gully.
  • Trash is separated and placed in containers — not placed next to full tons. And please never burn in a campfire: plastic in the fire poisons the air and soil. Used diapers and wet wipes should be securely sealed, exclusively in the residual waste.

Rule 4: Dogs on a leash, bag in hand

Camping with a dog is wonderful and widespread—as long as a few basic rules apply: respect the leash requirement on the site, don't leave your dog unattended and barking at the camper, clean up shops anytime and anywhere, and accept that not everyone loves dogs. Many places have their own dog showers and outdoor areas — use them.

Rule 5: Helpfulness and Community

The camper community thrives on mutual support, and that's exactly what gives them their charm. Instructing when maneuvering, lending tools or a pinch of salt, pointing out newcomers to the available space, sharing tips about the region and route — all of this goes without saying. A friendly greeting as you pass by is just as much a part of it as a quick chat in the evening, if both sides like.

Equally important is the sense of the opposite direction: not everyone seeks company. Anyone who reads his book or clearly wants peace and quiet will get it. The art lies in a balance of openness and respect.

Rule 6: Protect Nature — Fire, Food, and Wildlife

The closeness to nature is the most beautiful thing about camping. Let's make sure it stays that way:

  • Only take photos and memories with you: do not break branches, do not damage trees for firewood, do not pick rare plants.
  • Be careful with fire: Barbecue and campfire bans apply without exception. Never leave a fire unattended and put out completely before you go to sleep or leave.
  • Storing food safely: Food smells attract wildlife. Food belongs in the vehicle or in fixed stalls — and wild animals are never fed, only observed from a safe distance.
  • Washing up & showering outdoors: Keep at least 50 meters distance from streams, rivers, and lakes and use only minimal amounts of biodegradable soap.

Rule 7: When standing freely, you are an ambassador

Outside official venues, you represent the entire camper community — whether you want to or not. Every misconduct pays into the "prohibition sign" account, every exemplary behavior keeps the doors open. Therefore, the stricter version of etiquette applies here:

  • Remain unobtrusive: no furniture, no grill, no extended awning.
  • One night, then move on.
  • "Pack it in, pack it out": leave no trash or traces — if in doubt, also take other people's waste with you.
  • Dispose of wastewater and toilets exclusively at official stations.
  • Residents are kindly greeted and respond without discussion when asked to continue.

I've written about how this looks specifically in Poland — where overnight stays in vehicles are allowed and where it isn't — in a separate post: Where is it legal to stay overnight in Poland? →

Conclusion: Camping etiquette is not a rigid set of rules, but a practiced consideration: respect space, peace, and cleanliness, be helpful without being intrusive, and behave outdoors in nature like a guest who wants to return. Those who bring this attitude do not need to memorize a single rule — and will be welcomed with open arms in every square in Europe.
