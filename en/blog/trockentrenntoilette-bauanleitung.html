The dry separation toilet in campers: Why it is the best solution – including assembly instructions

July 2026 · 9 min. reading time · from your Tussy

Anyone who converts their vehicle into a camper sooner or later faces a fundamental question: Where to go with the human business? Chemical toilets, with their blue additives and the unpleasant emptying process, were long the standard solution. But more and more campers are discovering a low-odor, environmentally friendly, and surprisingly simple alternative: the dry separation toilet. In this article, you'll learn how it works, what advantages it offers – and how you can build one yourself with manageable effort.

What exactly is a dry separation toilet?

The principle is simple but ingenious: urine and solids are collected separately before they can mix. This very mixture is the main cause of unpleasant odors in conventional camping toilets. Urine alone hardly smells as long as it is fresh. Solids, in turn, only develop that typical "toilet smell" when combined with moisture and without oxygen supply. When both components are kept separate, it remains surprisingly odorless.

The heart of every dry separation toilet is the so-called urine separator—an insert at the front of the seat surface that directs urine into a separate canister or tank. The solids fall through a larger opening behind it into a bucket or container equipped with a dry substrate such as bark mulch, coconut fibers, sawdust, or litter. This substrate binds moisture and speeds up composting without the need for an active decomposition process inside the vehicle itself—which the local compost heap takes care of later.

The advantages over the chemical toilet

The most obvious advantage is the avoidance of chemicals. Classic camping toilets require sanitary fluid to neutralize odors and chemically treat the feces in the tank. These substances are not only a recurring cost factor, but must also be disposed of properly—not every motorhome pitch offers a suitable disposal station for this.

With the dry separation toilet, this is completely omitted. The urine canister can be easily emptied in any regular toilet or even in nature (far from bodies of water), since untreated urine is largely harmless. The solids in the compost bin need to be emptied much less often – depending on usage intensity, a change every one to two weeks is sufficient, as no water or chemicals inflates the volume.

In addition: dry separation toilets do not require a water connection or flushing. This saves valuable fresh water reserves – a real argument, especially for self-sufficient sites without water supply. Weight also plays a role: a self-built system often weighs less than a filled chemical toilet cassette tank.

What you need for DIY

The good news: A functioning dry separation toilet can be built in a weekend with basic tools and just a few materials. You will need:

  • A urine separator (made of plastic, available cheaply in specialist stores or online)
  • A sturdy bucket with a lid for the solids, ideally 10–20 liters capacity
  • A canister or bottle with a hose connection for urine
  • Multiplex or screen printing plates (at least 12 mm thick) for the enclosure
  • A toilet seat with a lid that fits the housing dimensions
  • Silicone or wood glue for sealing the seams
  • Screws, angles, and, if applicable, edge tape
  • Optional: a small 12-volt fan for active ventilation
  • Substrate such as coconut humus, wood shavings, or bark mulch

Step-by-step construction instructions

  1. Take measurements and plan. First, determine the available space in your camper. A typical dry separation toilet requires a base area of about 35 x 40 cm and a height of 35–40 cm so that the solid waste container fits comfortably. Sketch the case as a simple box with a removable or flip-open lid.
  2. Trim the housing. Cut the side walls, floor, and top panel from the plywood panels. The cover plate has two cutouts: a larger rear opening for the solids and a smaller front opening for the urine separator. The easiest way is to transfer the separator's dimensions directly onto the wood with a pen before cutting with the jigsaw.
  3. Glue and screw the housing. Connect the side walls to the floor using angles or tenon joints, and glue the edges additionally for stability. Make sure the insides are smooth and free of edges where substrate could get stuck later.
  4. Install urine separator. Place the separator in the front recess and seal it all around with silicone to prevent urine from leaking inside the housing. From the separator's outlet nozzle, you lead a flexible hose down to the canister. Important: The hose should have a slight slope so that urine drains reliably and does not accumulate.
  5. Position canisters and solid containers. Install a small holder or compartment for the urine canister so it doesn't slip while driving. The solid bin should remain removable so you can easily pull it out to empty it.
  6. Install ventilation (optional, but recommended). Drill a small hole in the back wall and install a 12-volt fan there that vents outward via a hose. This further reduces residual moisture in the solid container and ensures an even more odorless result.
  7. Install the lid and seat surface. Screw the toilet seat onto the cover plate so that both openings are cleanly covered. A lid with a good fit is crucial to reliably contain odors even when closed.
  8. Seal the surface. Treat the entire wooden enclosure with a moisture-resistant varnish or clear coat. This protects against splashes and makes later cleaning much easier.

Care and use in everyday life

Before first use, add a layer of substrate to the solid container. After every "big store," sprinkle a handful of dry material over it – this binds moisture and reliably keeps odors out. The urine canister should be emptied regularly, ideally every two to three days, depending on the number of people and the size of the canister. As mentioned, the solid waste container can remain standing much longer and is then disposed of via household waste, a composting toilet station, or—if appropriate post-composting—your own garden.

Conclusion

A dry separation toilet is more than just a trend among vanliers: it elegantly solves the practical problems of the classic camping toilet—less chemicals, less odor, less water consumption, and greater independence from disposal stations. With some craftsmanship and a weekend of time, the system can be built precisely for your own camper. Once you've switched, you rarely want to go back to the chemical toilet—and for good reason.
