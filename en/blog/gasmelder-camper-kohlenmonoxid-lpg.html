Gas detectors in campers: Which detector really protects?

September 6, 2026 · approx. 13 min. reading time · from your Tussy

A small box is supposed to watch over our lives in our sleep. That sounds reassuring – but it only works if the sensor really fits the throttle. Liquefied gas, carbon monoxide, and the often promoted "anesthetic gas" are three different topics. That's why I ran the 15-device lab test from Motorhome International 02/2025 and 03/2025 and compared with the current standards and safety instructions.

⚠️ First things first A gas detector is an additional protection, but neither a repair tool nor a free pass. It does not replace proper installation, ventilation, or mandatory gas testing. In case of an alarm, all persons leave the camper immediately. In case of CO alarm, complaints, or unclear situation: stay outside and call 112.
Summary for those in a hurry
  • For propane and butane, you need a certified LPG sensor.
  • For carbon monoxide, you need a own CO sensor; a broadband LPG sensor is not a reliable replacement.
  • Check the EU Declaration of Conformity DIN EN 50194-2 for flammable gases or DIN EN 50291-2 for CO in recreational vehicles.
  • LPG detectors are usually installed in depth, and CO detectors in the area of the breathing or sleeping zone – the instructions for the specific device remain crucial.
  • An "anesthesia gas detector" cannot guarantee an unknown list of all conceivable substances. Treat this feature as an add-on, not as intrusion protection.

Three dangers that are often lumped together

LPG

Propane & Butane

Flammable liquefied gas can leak from bottles, hoses, regulators, or lines. A detector for LPG or flammable gases is being sought.

CO

carbon monoxide

The toxic combustion product is colorless and odorless. It requires a specially designed CO sensor and a different mounting position.

?

"Anesthetic gas"

This is not a single, technically clearly defined substance. A device can only detect the substances for which its sensor is designed and tested.

1. LPG: When propane or butane leaks

In campers, liquefied gas often supplies stoves, heaters, or refrigerators. If propane or butane escapes unburned, in addition to the risk of suffocation, there is above all a risk of fire and explosion. Both gases tend to accumulate in deep areas. Therefore, according to the manufacturer's manual, an LPG sensor is usually placed close to the ground and not somewhere comfortably at eye level.

For permanently installed detectors in recreational vehicles, the DIN EN 50194-2:2020-08 the important reference. It describes test procedures and performance requirements for devices that detect LPG or gasoline vapors and provide visual and acoustic warnings. A reference to this standard in the current EU Declaration of Conformity is more meaningful than a product page full of buzzwords.

Important: The detector should detect escaping gas before a critical concentration is reached. However, it does not prevent the leak. Bottle valve, pressure regulator, hose, pipes, and devices still need to be in good condition.

2. CO: The invisible danger of combustion

Carbon monoxide is produced during incomplete combustion – for example, due to a defective device, impaired exhaust pipes, or unsuitable combustion devices in the interior. CO can neither be smelled nor seen. That's exactly why an audible CO warning in the small sleeping area of a camper is so important.

The DIN EN 50291-2:2020-10 deals with CO warning devices for continuous operation in recreational vehicles and similar environments, including recreational boats. For selection, installation, use, and maintenance, the current guide is also available DIN EN 50292:2024-07.

A common misconception is: A sensor that detects propane and butane will somehow capture CO as well. In the RMI test, several such advertised broadband sensors failed at CO, while separate CO sensors from the same systems, specifically offered for this purpose, worked. My clear conclusion: If CO is on the packaging, there must also be a real, designated CO sensor behind it.

3. What about "anesthetic gas"?

Stories of travelers allegedly drugged with gas during a break-in persist. The problem already starts with the term: "Anesthetic gas" does not name a clear substance. However, a sensor cannot respond to a word, only to certain chemical compounds or groups of substances.

For the follow-up, RMI used trichloroethylene and chose 240 ppm as the test concentration. The editorial team itself emphasizes that there is no specific DIN testing standard for general "anesthetic gas" sensors. The accompanying article also distinguishes between two situations: A large amount of gas that silently and precisely puts awake people to sleep is considered not very plausible; a low concentration in those already asleep could theoretically have a different effect. According to the editorial team, there was no hard evidence of concrete robberies.

That's why I would never make this additional feature the main criterion. It can be interesting if a reliable LPG detector also detects certain solvent vapors. However, door security, an alarm system, a secure place to stay overnight, and your own behavior help against burglary. A gas detector does not replace these measures.

What the 2025 RMI lab test actually tested

The special print you photographed contains two publications Motorhome International 02/2025 and 03/2025 together. Fifteen devices or variants as well as additional sensors were tested. In addition to the reaction to propane/butane, carbon monoxide, and later trichloroethylene, the editorial team considered, among other things, power consumption, alarm volume, self-testing, error display, labeling, and installation instructions.

For LPG and CO, the editorial team followed the concentration and timing requirements of the relevant standards. The anesthesia gas test, on the other hand, was an editorially developed supplementary test. What was particularly striking was not only, whether an alarm was triggered, but also whether a device reported a defective sensor and whether the manual led to the correct installation location.

Transparency in the test setup The test took place in Thitronik's development laboratory – and Thitronik itself was represented with products in comparison. RMI writes that all processes were standardized and reproducible, other manufacturers could participate and conduct control tests. The CO measurement technology was simultaneously tested by an independent certified expert. Nevertheless, this is a possible conflict of interest. Therefore, I treat the result as an important comparative test, not as the sole truth for every current product status.

My Buying Checklist for a Gas Detector

  1. Define hazard: Do you want to monitor LPG, CO, or both? Both require two suitable sensors or a system with separate sensor elements.
  2. Search vehicle standard: DIN EN 50194-2 for combustible gases and DIN EN 50291-2 for CO are specifically tailored for recreational vehicles.
  3. Read the EU Declaration of Conformity: Don't just look at a CE mark in a shop photo. The declaration should specify the specific model, the manufacturer, and the standards applied. The EU points out that the CE marking is fundamentally based on the manufacturer's declaration of responsibility.
  4. Check sensor type: For CO, explicitly require a special CO sensor. A vague "3-gas" or "universal" is not enough for me.
  5. Error display and lifespan: The device should clearly report a sensor fault and the end of the sensor lifespan. Check if the sensor or entire device needs to be replaced later.
  6. Being able to hear the alarm: The sound must reliably wake you up at bedtime. People with hearing impairments may need a compatible visual or vibrating additional warning.
  7. Realistically calculate electricity demand: In the RMI comparison, daily consumption varied widely. When standing independently, every Ah counts – but you still can't switch off the protection while sleeping.
  8. View original instructions before purchasing: If the mounting height, distances, warm-up time, test interval, cleaning, and replacement date are clearly stated, that's a good sign.

Mounting: The wrong place makes the best sensor useless

According to instructions, LPG detectors usually belong deep inside the interior because propane and butane sink downward. They must not disappear into the gas box if they are to monitor the breathing and living zones. Avoid blind spots, direct spray areas, and spots directly next to the stove, cleaning agents, or hairspray if the manufacturer expects false alarms there.

CO behaves differently and distributes with the room air. The CO detector belongs in or near the sleeping area, as the instructions for a camper prescribe. Simply mounting both sensors side by side on the floor just because it looks nice would not be a good solution.

Permanently connected devices require a permanently secure, secured supply. Mobile or battery-powered devices must be explicitly suitable for their intended use. Test the warning function at the intervals specified by the manufacturer and replace batteries, sensors, or devices on time. Please never "test" with lighter gas, exhaust gas, or an open flame.

What to do when the alarm goes off?

In case of LPG alarm or gas smell
  1. No flame, no smoking, and no operating electrical switches.
  2. Bring everyone outdoors immediately.
  3. Only if it is possible without delay and without risk: close the bottle valve and ventilate from the outside.
  4. In case of strong, persistent, or unclear danger, call 112.
  5. Only use the system again after professional examination.
In case of CO alarm
  1. Wake everyone immediately and leave the camper.
  2. Get outside and stay outside.
  3. Call 112 – especially for headaches, dizziness, nausea, dizziness, or shortness of breath.
  4. Do not go back until emergency personnel or experts give the all-clear.
  5. Have the cause professionally clarified before next use.

The German Firefighters Association In case of CO alarm, advise leaving immediately, calling emergency 112, and not going back in. This order applies the same in campers – the small interior can fill up especially quickly.

The detector does not replace the gas test

Since June 19, 2025, the recurring inspection of affected liquefied gas systems in German recreational vehicles has been mandatory. § 60 StVZO refers to DVGW worksheet G 607. Inspections are carried out before the first commissioning, after changes subject to inspection, and thereafter at the latest every 24 months. The DVGW explains: Pipes, equipment, and the entire system must be regularly inspected by experts.

This is not a duplication. The inspection is intended to prevent and identify deficiencies; the warning should also sound an alarm if gas or CO still occurs. Just like seat belts and brakes, both serve different functions.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need an LPG and a CO detector in the camper?

If you use a liquefied gas system and combustion devices, the combination makes sense. Choose either two suitable devices or a system with clearly separated LPG and CO sensors. A single broadband sensor with a blanket "for everything" doesn't convince me.

Does a smoke detector also detect carbon monoxide?

No. A smoke detector reacts to smoke particles, a CO detector to carbon monoxide. There are combination devices, but they must still contain separate sensor technologies designated for their purpose.

Does the test button prove that the gas sensor measures correctly?

Depending on the model, the test button checks electronics, display, and alarm; how comprehensively the sensor itself is monitored is stated in the manual. A button test does not replace automatic fault monitoring or timely sensor replacement.

Is the CE marking sufficient when buying?

It is important, but not an independent test seal. Have the current EU Declaration of Conformity shown to you and check whether your exact model and the appropriate recreational vehicle standard are listed there.

How long does a gas or CO detector last?

That depends on the sensor and the device. Look for manufacturing, replacement, or end-of-life information and stick to the shorter interval mentioned. An old sensor is not reliable just because the LED is still lit.

Conclusion: Not "one device for everything," but the right protection for every hazard

My most important result from the big test is surprisingly simple: When it comes to gas detectors, specialization counts more than a long list on the packaging. For propane and butane, a suitable LPG sensor belongs at the correct deep installation location. For carbon monoxide, a real CO sensor belongs in the sleeping area. And anyone who also wants to be warned about certain solvent vapors must carefully check which substances the manufacturer actually names and documents.

I would rather buy a comprehensibly documented system with two matching sensors than a cheap universal device with big promises. In addition, there is a gas test, free ventilation openings, well-maintained appliances, and an alarm plan that all passengers know. Safety in the van is not a single box – but a chain where every link must function.
